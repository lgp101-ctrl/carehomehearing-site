When to request a hearing assessment

Know when to ask for a hearing check, how residents can get one, and which changes need urgent medical advice instead.

Ask about hearing from the start

When a resident moves in, find out about their hearing and record it in their care plan:

  • Do they have hearing loss in one or both ears? Do they use hearing aids, a cochlear implant or other equipment?
  • Who supplied their hearing aids: the NHS or a private provider? Where are the contact details?
  • When was their last hearing test?
  • How do they prefer to communicate? Do they use British Sign Language (BSL), lip-reading or written notes?
  • Do they have any ear problems, such as frequent wax or infections?

Many homes use a hearing care passport to keep this information together. You can download one from the Hearing Care Toolkit.

When to ask for a routine hearing assessment

Ask the nurse, manager or GP about a hearing assessment when:

  • you or the family notice signs of hearing loss that have built up gradually
  • a hearing aid user seems to be hearing less well than before, even with working aids
  • the resident has never had a hearing test, or it has been a long time
  • the resident has, or may have, dementia or mild cognitive impairment
  • the resident has trouble with their hearing aids, such as discomfort or whistling, that simple checks have not fixed

How residents can get a hearing assessment

  • NHS: usually through a referral from the GP to the local NHS audiology service. In some areas, adults can be referred directly to NHS hearing services without seeing the GP first. Some NHS services can visit residents who cannot travel. Ask your GP practice or local NHS audiology service how it works in your area.
  • The existing provider: if the resident already has hearing aids, their NHS audiology service or private provider can review them.
  • Private hearing care providers: some residents or families choose a private provider. NHS and private services both have qualified professionals. It is the resident’s choice, with support from their family if needed.

Always involve the resident. If they may lack capacity to decide, follow the Mental Capacity Act and your care home’s policy for decisions in their best interests.

Do not book a routine test for these

Same day, contact the GP or NHS 111 if the resident:

  • has sudden hearing loss or a sudden worsening of hearing, in one or both ears
  • has ear pain with discharge, bleeding or swelling
  • has hearing loss with new dizziness or a spinning feeling
  • has hearing loss after a head injury (if they have hit their head and seem unwell, call 999)

Call 999

Call 999 if hearing loss comes with signs of a stroke: face drooping, weakness in an arm or leg, slurred or muddled speech, or sudden confusion.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting for the resident to ask for help
  • Assuming nothing can be done because the resident is very old or has dementia
  • Not recording who supplied the hearing aids
  • Booking a routine appointment for a sudden change

Check your understanding

Your answers are not recorded anywhere except, if possible, on this device.

When she woke up this morning, Mrs Hughes said she could hardly hear anything in her left ear. Yesterday her hearing was as usual.

What should happen?

Sudden hearing loss needs to be assessed urgently – NICE recommends that hearing loss which came on over 3 days or less in the past 30 days is seen by a specialist within 24 hours. Never assume sudden hearing loss is wax.

Which of these is a good reason to request a routine hearing assessment?

Gradual change is a reason for a routine hearing assessment. The other three need urgent or emergency medical help.
