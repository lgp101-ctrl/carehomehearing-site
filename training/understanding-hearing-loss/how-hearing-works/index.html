How hearing works

The ear collects sound, but it is the brain that makes sense of it. Knowing the three parts of the ear helps you understand what can go wrong.

We use our ears to collect sound, but we hear with our brain. The ears and the hearing nerve work together to send sound to the brain, which then works out what it means: a voice, a word, a fire alarm or a favourite song.

The three parts of the ear

The outer ear is the part you can see, plus the ear canal. It collects sound and funnels it towards the eardrum. The ear canal makes earwax, which protects the ear.

The middle ear is a small air-filled space behind the eardrum. When sound hits the eardrum, it vibrates. Three tiny bones, the smallest in the body, pass these vibrations on to the inner ear. A narrow tube called the Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the back of the throat and keeps the air pressure balanced.

The inner ear contains the cochlea, a snail-shaped tube filled with fluid and lined with thousands of tiny hair cells. The hair cells turn vibrations into electrical signals, which travel along the hearing nerve to the brain. The inner ear also contains the balance organs, which is why some ear problems cause dizziness.

Why this matters in a care home

Problems can happen in any part of this chain:

  • Something can block sound in the outer or middle ear, such as earwax or fluid from an infection.
  • The hair cells in the cochlea can wear out with age or be damaged by noise.
  • The brain may find it harder to process sound, for example after a stroke or with dementia.

Because each problem needs a different response, never guess the cause. Your job is to notice changes, support the resident to communicate, and make sure the right professional checks their ears and hearing.

Think about the whole person

A resident who “can hear but cannot understand” is common. They may hear that someone is speaking but find it hard to pick out the words, especially in a noisy room. Seeing your face, cutting background noise and giving extra time can make a big difference.

Which part of the ear turns sound vibrations into signals for the brain?

The cochlea is a snail-shaped organ in the inner ear. Thousands of tiny hair cells inside it turn vibrations into nerve signals that travel to the brain.

Why might a resident with dementia struggle to understand speech even when their ears are working?

We hear with our brains. Dementia can affect how the brain processes sound and language, so good communication support matters even when the ears themselves work well.
