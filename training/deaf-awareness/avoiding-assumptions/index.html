Avoiding assumptions about understanding and ability

A person who cannot hear you is not a person who cannot understand. Assumptions about hearing loss can lead to people being left out of their own care.

Hearing loss affects how well someone can receive information, not their intelligence or ability to make decisions. Yet people with hearing loss are often treated as if they are confused, and are left out.

Assumptions to avoid

  • “They are confused.” Mishearing can look like confusion. Check hearing and communication first.
  • “They can’t understand, so I’ll ask their family.” Speak to the resident. Family members can help, but only in the way the resident wants.
  • “They lack capacity.” Under the Mental Capacity Act 2005, a person must be assumed to have capacity unless it is shown otherwise, and they must be given all practicable help to make a decision. Communication support, such as a working hearing aid or an interpreter, is part of that help.
  • “Everyone with hearing loss can lip-read.” Lip-reading is a skill. Many speech sounds look the same on the lips, so even good lip-readers miss a lot.
  • “Shouting helps.” Shouting distorts your face and voice, can be uncomfortable for hearing aid users, and can seem aggressive.
  • “They heard me because they said yes.” People often nod or say yes to avoid embarrassment.

Never say “never mind”

When someone asks you to repeat, saying “never mind” or “it doesn’t matter” tells them they are not worth the effort. Rephrase instead, use fewer words, write it down or show them.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Talking about a resident in front of them as if they cannot hear
  • Leaving residents out of conversations at mealtimes or in activities
  • Treating communication difficulties as “behaviour”
  • Making decisions for a resident because conversation takes longer

Take the time

Good communication can take a few more minutes. Those minutes help the resident feel respected and involved, and they reduce mistakes in care.

Check your understanding

Your answers are not recorded anywhere except, if possible, on this device.

During a care review, the visiting nurse asks Mr Clarke's daughter questions about his pain, while Mr Clarke, who has hearing loss, sits beside her.

What would be most respectful?

Speak to the resident, not their companion. Make sure he can hear or see what is being said, and involve his daughter only in the way he wants.

A resident nods and smiles when you speak, but you are not sure they heard you. What should you do?

Nodding is often a way to avoid embarrassment. An open question such as "What would you like for lunch?" checks understanding more reliably than "Did you hear me?"
