TV listeners, hearing loops and group listening

How residents can enjoy TV, films, talks and services without everything being turned up for everyone.

TV listening systems

A TV listener has a small transmitter connected to the TV and headphones or a neckloop for the resident. The resident sets their own volume, so the TV can stay at a comfortable level for others.

  • Many modern hearing aids can stream TV sound directly using a TV streamer accessory. Ask the hearing aid supplier.
  • Subtitles should be offered as standard. Most TVs have a subtitles button on the remote.

Hearing loops

A hearing loop is a wire around a room, or a small counter loop at a reception desk, connected to an amplifier. It sends sound as a magnetic signal that hearing aids can pick up when switched to the T (loop) programme.

  • The hearing aid must have a T programme, and it must be switched on. The supplier can tell you, and can often add one.
  • Check the loop is switched on and working regularly, for example with a simple loop listener. Record the checks.
  • Display the loop sign where a loop is fitted.
  • Residents without hearing aids can use a loop listener with headphones.

Group listening systems

For talks, quizzes, worship and entertainment, a group listening system uses a microphone for the speaker and wireless receivers with headphones or neckloops for listeners. It is also useful when a room cannot have a loop installed.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming a loop works without ever checking it
  • Assuming every hearing aid has a loop programme switched on
  • Turning the lounge TV up very loud instead of offering a TV listener
  • Leaving subtitles off because “nobody asked”

Check your understanding

A hearing loop is fitted in the lounge, but a hearing aid user says it makes no difference. What should you check?

A loop only helps if the hearing aid has a T (loop) programme switched on and the loop amplifier is on and working. The supplier can add a T programme to many hearing aids.
