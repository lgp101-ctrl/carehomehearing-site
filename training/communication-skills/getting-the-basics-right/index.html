Getting the basics right

Five simple habits make the biggest difference to residents with hearing loss. They take seconds and become second nature with practice.

1. Check hearing aids first

Before an important conversation, check that hearing aids are in, switched on and working. If the resident wears glasses, check they are clean and on, too. Lip-reading needs good eyesight.

2. Get their attention before you speak

  • Say their name first.
  • If they cannot see you, move into their line of sight, or wave gently.
  • A light touch on the arm or shoulder can help, if the resident is comfortable with that. Check their care plan.
  • Do not start talking from behind or from another room.

3. Face them

  • Be at the same eye level: sit or kneel rather than standing over them.
  • Stay about one metre away, close enough to see your face, not so close that it is uncomfortable.
  • Keep your hands, cups, pens and masks away from your mouth. If your infection control policy requires masks, ask whether clear-panel masks can be used.
  • If they hear better on one side, sit on that side, but stay in view.

4. Speak clearly and naturally

  • Speak at a normal pace, slightly slower if needed, with natural rhythm.
  • Raise your voice a little if needed, but do not shout.
  • Do not exaggerate your lip movements; this makes lip-reading harder.
  • Use short, simple sentences and put the key word first: “Lunch is ready” rather than “I just wanted to let you know that it’s lunchtime now”.

5. Rephrase, do not just repeat

If something is not understood after one repeat, say it differently. Some words are easier to hear or lip-read than others. “Would you like a drink?” might work better as “Tea or coffee?” with a gesture towards a cup.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Talking while walking away, or while looking at a screen or notes
  • Speaking from behind, or from a doorway
  • Shouting or over-mouthing words
  • Saying “never mind” when you are not understood

Why is shouting unhelpful for someone with hearing loss?

Shouting distorts the sounds of speech and the shape of your lips, and can make hearing aids uncomfortable. It can also upset people. Speak clearly at a slightly raised, natural level instead.

What should you try next?

Some words and sounds are harder to hear or lip-read. Using different words, fewer words and a gesture or object often works when repeating does not.
