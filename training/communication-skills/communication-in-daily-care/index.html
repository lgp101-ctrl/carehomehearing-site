Communication in daily care, emergencies and at the end of life

Seven everyday situations, each with practical tips. Use these as a checklist in your own home.

1. Personal care

  • Explain before you start and before each step, while the resident can see your face. Do this before removing hearing aids for washing.
  • Agree simple signals (for example, a thumbs up for “OK” or “stop”) for when hearing aids are out.
  • Put hearing aids back in as soon as possible afterwards.

2. Medication rounds

  • Make sure the resident can see and hear you before explaining any medicine.
  • Show the tablet or medicine and name it clearly.
  • Write down changes to doses or new medicines.
  • Check understanding with an open question: “What is this one for?”
  • Medication rounds are often noisy. Step aside to a quieter spot for important explanations.

3. Mealtimes

  • Seat residents with hearing loss away from the kitchen, serving hatch and doors, and where they can see others’ faces.
  • Use small tables where possible; large busy tables are hard to follow.
  • Show menus or plated choices rather than reading choices out.
  • Turn background music down or off.

4. Family visits

  • Check hearing aids are in and working before visitors arrive.
  • Suggest a quiet room or corner with good light.
  • Share tips with families: face the person, one person speaks at a time, rephrase rather than shout.
  • Video calls: turn on captions and use headphones or a hearing aid connection if available.

5. Group activities

  • Seat residents with hearing loss near the leader and where they can see faces.
  • Only one person speaks at a time; repeat questions from the group before answering.
  • Use the hearing loop or a microphone if you have one, and visuals: pictures, large-print words, objects.
  • Keep background music off during talking parts.

6. Emergencies

  • Make sure each resident’s personal emergency evacuation plan (PEEP) shows whether they can hear alarms and how they will be alerted, for example by being woken in person, and with visual or vibrating alerts if fitted.
  • In an emergency, approach from the front, get their attention, use clear gestures and short phrases (“Fire. Come with me.”), and keep them with you.
  • When calling 999 or speaking to paramedics, tell them about the hearing loss and bring the hearing aids or hearing passport if there is time.

7. End-of-life care

  • Many people can still hear in their final days, even if they cannot respond.
  • Keep hearing aids in if comfortable and if the resident would want them. Check they are working.
  • Speak gently, say who you are, and tell them what you are doing.
  • Encourage family to talk, read or play favourite music. Touch, such as holding a hand, also communicates comfort.
  • Make sure advance care planning conversations are fully accessible, with interpreters or hearing support, while the resident can take part.

The fire alarm sounds at night. Mr Shah, who is profoundly deaf, is asleep with his hearing aids out.

Which approach is right?

Residents who cannot hear alarms need a planned approach, recorded in their personal emergency evacuation plan, such as being woken in person and visual or vibrating alerts.

Mrs Murphy is in the last days of life. She has worn hearing aids for twenty years. Her family ask whether to keep talking to her.

What is the best advice?

Many people can still hear near the end of life. Gentle talking, familiar voices and music can comfort, and hearing aids can stay in if comfortable and in line with her wishes.
