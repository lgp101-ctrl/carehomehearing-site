Using writing, pictures and captions

Speech is not the only way to communicate. Simple visual tools help residents follow what is happening, especially for important information.

Writing things down

  • Keep a notepad and pen, or a small whiteboard, in the resident’s room and in communal areas.
  • Write clearly, in large print, using a dark pen.
  • Use key words, not long sentences.
  • Write down information that is easy to mishear: times, dates, names, numbers and medication changes.
  • Remember that some residents, including some BSL users and people with dementia, may find written English hard. Check what works.

Pictures, symbols and gestures

  • Communication cards or boards with pictures for common needs, such as drink, toilet, pain, food, too hot or too cold, help residents point to what they want.
  • Natural gestures, such as miming drinking or pointing to a watch, support speech.
  • Objects of reference, such as showing a towel before a bath, can help residents with dementia.
  • Photos of family, staff and places can make conversations easier.

Captions and speech-to-text

  • Turn on subtitles on televisions and video calls. Most TVs and video call apps have them.
  • Free speech-to-text apps on phones and tablets show spoken words as text in real time. They can help in one-to-one conversations, family visits and meetings. Check accuracy, as they can make mistakes, and do not use them for confidential conversations unless your organisation has approved it.
  • Some phones can caption phone and video calls automatically.

Care home scenario

A whiteboard on the wall

Mrs Chen has severe hearing loss and is learning to use hearing aids. Her care team put a small whiteboard by her bed. Each morning staff write the day, date, the names of staff on shift and any appointments. Mrs Chen says it helps her “know where I am in the day” and she uses it to write questions for the nurse.

Accessible information is a requirement, not an extra

Providing information in a way people can understand is part of good care and, in England, part of the Accessible Information Standard.

Check your understanding

Your answers are not recorded anywhere except, if possible, on this device.

Which kind of information is most important to write down for a resident with hearing loss?

Numbers, names, dates and times are easily misheard. Writing them down avoids mistakes and gives the resident something to refer back to.
