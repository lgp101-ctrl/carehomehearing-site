Hearing-Friendly Care Home self-assessment

Answer 33 short questions across seven areas to see where your home is doing well and get a personalised improvement plan. It takes about 10 minutes and works best completed by the manager with the Hearing Loss Champion.

This is a self-assessment. It is not an independent assessment, inspection or certification, and completing it does not mean your care home is accredited or approved by anyone.

Unanswered questions are treated as "not yet in place". Your answers are saved on this device only.

1. Staff knowledge

Do staff have the knowledge and confidence to support residents' hearing?

All care staff have completed basic hearing care training.
New staff receive hearing care training as part of their induction.
Staff know how to put in, take out and clean each type of hearing aid used in the home.
Staff recognise the signs of hearing loss and know when and how to escalate concerns.
Hearing care training is refreshed at least once a year and completions are recorded.

2. Hearing aid maintenance

Are residents' hearing aids clean, working and worn?

Every resident's hearing aids are checked daily to make sure they are in, working, clean and comfortable.
The right batteries are always in stock, and chargers are working and switched on.
Hearing aids are kept in labelled cases in an agreed place, written in the care plan.
Tubing, domes and wax filters are checked and replaced when needed.
Lost hearing aids are recorded as incidents and reviewed to prevent repeats.

3. Referral procedures

Do residents get the right help, at the right time, from the right service?

Staff know that sudden hearing loss, ear pain, discharge or new dizziness need medical advice the same day.
There is a clear, agreed route for arranging hearing assessments and ear examinations.
Each hearing aid user's supplier (NHS service or private provider) and contact details are recorded.
Hearing needs and hearing aids are included in hospital transfer information.
Hearing is asked about when residents move in and reviewed at care reviews.

4. Communication practices

Can residents with hearing loss take part in conversations and decisions?

Each resident's communication needs and preferences are recorded and followed.
Staff get residents' attention, face them and speak clearly without shouting.
Notepads, whiteboards, pictures or captions are available and used where helpful.
Registered BSL interpreters are booked for important conversations where needed.
Residents living with dementia and hearing loss have individual communication plans.

5. Acoustic environment

Is the building easy to hear in?

There is at least one quiet space for conversations and visits.
TVs, radios and music are turned down or off when no one is actively enjoying them.
Lounges and dining rooms have soft furnishings that reduce echo.
Lighting is good, including in the evening, so residents can see faces.
Fire and emergency alerts are suitable for residents who cannot hear alarms.

6. Assistive listening equipment

Is technology used to help residents hear?

Personal listening amplifiers are available for residents without hearing aids.
Subtitles and TV listeners are offered to residents who struggle with the TV.
Hearing loops are fitted where useful and checked regularly.
Shared equipment is cleaned between users and kept charged.

7. Family involvement

Are families partners in hearing care?

Families are asked about their relative's hearing and hearing aids when they move in.
Families know who to talk to about hearing care.
Families are given practical tips for communicating during visits.
Families are kept informed about hearing aid problems, losses and appointments.
