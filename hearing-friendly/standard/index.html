The Hearing-Friendly Care Home standard

33 criteria in seven areas, based on RNID's guidance for care homes and NICE guideline NG98. The standard is free to read and use. Any home can work towards it with the free self-assessment, which asks the same questions.

Draft for review. This standard is waiting for review by a qualified audiologist and the advisory panel. No home can be verified against it until the review is complete.

How independent verification works · Printable assessor checklist

1. Staff knowledge

Do staff have the knowledge and confidence to support residents' hearing?

  1. All care staff have completed basic hearing care training. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Training records show every care staff member has completed basic hearing care training; staff on shift confirm it.

  2. New staff receive hearing care training as part of their induction.

    What the assessor looks for: The induction programme includes hearing care, and recent starters' records show they completed it.

  3. Staff know how to put in, take out and clean each type of hearing aid used in the home. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Two or three staff show how they put in, take out and clean the types of hearing aid residents use.

  4. Staff recognise the signs of hearing loss and know when and how to escalate concerns.

    What the assessor looks for: Staff describe the signs of hearing loss and the home's routine, urgent and emergency routes for concerns.

  5. Hearing care training is refreshed at least once a year and completions are recorded.

    What the assessor looks for: Records show hearing care training refreshed within the last 12 months and completions logged.

2. Hearing aid maintenance

Are residents' hearing aids clean, working and worn?

  1. Every resident's hearing aids are checked daily to make sure they are in, working, clean and comfortable. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Daily check records for hearing aid users are complete; aids seen on residents are in, working and clean.

  2. The right batteries are always in stock, and chargers are working and switched on.

    What the assessor looks for: The right batteries are in stock and in date; chargers are working and in use.

  3. Hearing aids are kept in labelled cases in an agreed place, written in the care plan.

    What the assessor looks for: Hearing aids are kept in labelled cases in the place each care plan states.

  4. Tubing, domes and wax filters are checked and replaced when needed.

    What the assessor looks for: Tubing, domes and wax filters seen on aids are in good condition; spares are available or ordered.

  5. Lost hearing aids are recorded as incidents and reviewed to prevent repeats.

    What the assessor looks for: Lost hearing aids are logged as incidents, with actions taken to prevent repeats.

3. Referral procedures

Do residents get the right help, at the right time, from the right service?

  1. Staff know that sudden hearing loss, ear pain, discharge or new dizziness need medical advice the same day. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Staff say that sudden hearing loss, ear pain, discharge or new dizziness need medical advice the same day; the escalation guide is on display.

  2. There is a clear, agreed route for arranging hearing assessments and ear examinations.

    What the assessor looks for: A written route for arranging hearing assessments and ear examinations is known to senior staff and has been used.

  3. Each hearing aid user's supplier (NHS service or private provider) and contact details are recorded. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Each hearing aid user's NHS service or private provider and contact details are recorded in their care plan.

  4. Hearing needs and hearing aids are included in hospital transfer information.

    What the assessor looks for: Hospital transfer information includes hearing needs and hearing aids; an example transfer form is seen.

  5. Hearing is asked about when residents move in and reviewed at care reviews.

    What the assessor looks for: Admission assessments ask about hearing, and care reviews show hearing reviewed.

4. Communication practices

Can residents with hearing loss take part in conversations and decisions?

  1. Each resident's communication needs and preferences are recorded and followed. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Communication needs and preferences are recorded in care plans and match what staff describe and do.

  2. Staff get residents' attention, face them and speak clearly without shouting.

    What the assessor looks for: Staff are seen getting residents' attention, facing them and speaking clearly without shouting.

  3. Notepads, whiteboards, pictures or captions are available and used where helpful.

    What the assessor looks for: Notepads, whiteboards, pictures or captions are available in shared areas and used where helpful.

  4. Registered BSL interpreters are booked for important conversations where needed.

    What the assessor looks for: The home knows how to book a registered BSL interpreter and has done so where needed.

  5. Residents living with dementia and hearing loss have individual communication plans.

    What the assessor looks for: Residents living with dementia and hearing loss have individual communication plans that staff follow.

5. Acoustic environment

Is the building easy to hear in?

  1. There is at least one quiet space for conversations and visits. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: At least one quiet space is available and used for conversations and visits.

  2. TVs, radios and music are turned down or off when no one is actively enjoying them.

    What the assessor looks for: During the visit, TVs, radios and music are off or low when no one is watching or listening.

  3. Lounges and dining rooms have soft furnishings that reduce echo.

    What the assessor looks for: Lounges and dining rooms have soft furnishings that reduce echo.

  4. Lighting is good, including in the evening, so residents can see faces.

    What the assessor looks for: Lighting in shared areas lets residents see faces clearly, including in the evening.

  5. Fire and emergency alerts are suitable for residents who cannot hear alarms. Key criterion

    What the assessor looks for: Residents who cannot hear alarms have visual or vibrating alerts, or a written personal evacuation plan that covers this.

6. Assistive listening equipment

Is technology used to help residents hear?

  1. Personal listening amplifiers are available for residents without hearing aids.

    What the assessor looks for: Personal listening amplifiers are available, working and offered to residents without hearing aids.

  2. Subtitles and TV listeners are offered to residents who struggle with the TV.

    What the assessor looks for: Subtitles or TV listeners are offered and in use for residents who struggle with the TV.

  3. Hearing loops are fitted where useful and checked regularly.

    What the assessor looks for: Hearing loops, where fitted, work when tested, and checks are recorded.

  4. Shared equipment is cleaned between users and kept charged.

    What the assessor looks for: Shared listening equipment is clean and charged, with a cleaning routine in place.

7. Family involvement

Are families partners in hearing care?

  1. Families are asked about their relative's hearing and hearing aids when they move in.

    What the assessor looks for: Admission records show families are asked about their relative's hearing and hearing aids.

  2. Families know who to talk to about hearing care.

    What the assessor looks for: Information given to families names who to talk to about hearing care.

  3. Families are given practical tips for communicating during visits.

    What the assessor looks for: Families are given practical tips for communicating during visits, for example a leaflet or a poster.

  4. Families are kept informed about hearing aid problems, losses and appointments.

    What the assessor looks for: Records show families are told about hearing aid problems, losses and appointments.
