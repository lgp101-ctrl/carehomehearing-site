Why hearing care matters

In most care homes there is at least one resident who has quietly stopped joining in. Often the cause isn't dementia or low mood: they simply can't hear. When hearing goes, people withdraw, become more confused, fall more often and lose touch with the people around them. Most of this is preventable with a few everyday routines.

  • 80% of residents in care homes for older people live with hearing loss. (Engage, Care England and Nightingale Hammerson, 2025)
  • 1 in 4 Fewer than one in four people who could benefit from hearing aids use them. (As cited in the 2025 paper)
  • 27% of care home staff said they check or test residents' hearing aids. (Cross et al., 2024)
  • 3× people with hearing loss are around three times more likely to fall. (As cited in the 2025 paper)

The Hearing Friendly Care Homes campaign

  1. Sign up. Your home joins the campaign and names at least one Hearing Champion.
  2. Train. Your team uses the free Training Academy, or books on-site Hearing Champion training. Training from any provider counts.
  3. Get verified. An independent assessor checks your home against the published standard. Verified homes display the badge for 12 months.

Your role as a Hearing Champion

A Hearing Champion is a member of staff who leads hearing care day to day. You don't need to be an expert. You need to care about residents' hearing, know the basics well and help others do the same.

  1. Encourage daily hearing aid checks on every shift.
  2. Support colleagues with hearing aid care and communication.
  3. Identify residents who need professional support, such as a hearing assessment or ear examination.
  4. Maintain hearing care equipment: listening kits, TV listeners, loops, batteries and spares.
  5. Coordinate referrals with the nurse, GP, audiology and suppliers.
  6. Monitor hearing accessibility around the building.
  7. Help embed hearing care into daily routines, care plans and induction.

You are not expected to examine ears, remove wax, diagnose or repair hearing aids. Urgent problems always go to the nurse in charge, the GP, NHS 111 or 999.

Your routine

Getting started

  • Complete Hearing Champion training
  • Agree time for the role with your manager
  • Complete the Hearing Friendly Care Home self-assessment with your manager
  • List every resident with hearing needs, their equipment and supplier
  • Introduce yourself to residents, families and colleagues

Every week

  • Complete the weekly hearing care checklist
  • Follow up any hearing aid problems or referrals
  • Check equipment, batteries and chargers
  • Spend time on the floor helping colleagues

Every month

  • Review lost hearing aid incidents and agree actions
  • Run a 10-minute team huddle on one hearing care topic
  • Check new residents have a hearing passport
  • Update the manager on progress and problems

Every year

  • Repeat the self-assessment and environment audit
  • Check staff training records and arrange refreshers
  • Review local contacts: GP practice, NHS audiology and other hearing care providers, interpreters

The one-minute morning check

Do this for every resident who uses hearing aids, every morning, before breakfast.

  1. Find. Take the hearing aids from their named place: the labelled case or the charger.
  2. Power. Is it charged, or does it have a fresh battery with the door closed? A new zinc-air battery works best if left a minute after the tab is removed.
  3. Clean. Wipe with a dry cloth or the manufacturer's wipe. Never use water or alcohol. Check the dome and wax filter are clear.
  4. Listen. Switch on and cup the aid in your hand. A whistle usually means it's working.
  5. Insert. Red or R goes in the right ear, blue or L in the left. Insert gently and check it feels comfortable.
  6. Check. Ask a simple question in your normal voice. Note any problem and tell the Hearing Champion.

Speaking so residents can follow

  • Get the person's attention before you start speaking.
  • Face them, at their eye level, with light on your face, not behind you.
  • Turn off the TV or radio, or move somewhere quieter.
  • Speak clearly at a normal pace. Don't shout: it distorts speech and looks angry.
  • If they don't follow, rephrase rather than repeat the same words.
  • One person speaks at a time in groups and at mealtimes.
  • Sit on their better-hearing side, and use gestures, objects or writing to help.
  • Check they've understood, especially for care decisions and medication.

Environment checklist

Walk through the home with your manager and tick each item that's in place. Repeat once a year.

Lounge and activity areas

  • TV and radio are off when no one is watching or listening
  • Seating is in small groups, away from doors, kitchens and corridors
  • Lighting is even, and no one sits with their back to a bright window when talking
  • The hearing loop (if fitted) works and is signed
  • Listening equipment is charged and easy to find

Dining room

  • Background music is off at mealtimes
  • Soft furnishings, tablecloths or felt chair pads reduce clatter
  • Residents with hearing loss sit away from the kitchen and serving trolleys
  • Staff face residents when offering choices

Bedrooms and hearing aid care

  • Each resident has a labelled case or charger in a set place
  • Spare batteries, domes and wax filters are stocked and in date
  • Hearing needs are recorded in the care plan or hearing passport
  • A quiet room is available for family visits and phone calls

Questions care homes ask

How do you look after hearing aids in a care home?

Check every resident's hearing aids each morning: charged or with a working battery, clean, dome and wax filter clear, and in the right ear (red or R for right, blue or L for left). Wipe them with a dry cloth or the manufacturer's wipe, never water or alcohol. At night, store them in the labelled case or charger. Record any problem and tell the Hearing Champion.

What should carers check when a hearing aid isn't working?

Use a four-step check. 1) Power: is it charged, or the battery fresh and the battery door closed? 2) Blockage: is the wax filter, dome or tube blocked? 3) Fit: is it in the correct ear and pushed fully in? 4) The ear: does the resident have signs of earwax? If it still doesn't work, contact the audiologist; a loan aid can be provided during repairs.

How can care staff tell if a resident has a hearing problem?

Common signs are turning the TV up, asking people to repeat, answering the wrong question, withdrawing from groups or meals, watching faces closely, saying people mumble, or seeming more confused than usual. Any of these is a reason to request a hearing check.

What are the signs of earwax in a care home resident?

Muffled hearing, a blocked or full feeling, a hearing aid that whistles or sounds weak, or wax visible on the dome or filter. Don't use cotton buds. Book a professional ear check and microsuction earwax removal if needed.

What should a care home do when a hearing aid is lost?

Search bedding, clothing, laundry, food trays and chair cushions straight away, record the incident, and tell the family and the audiologist. Labelling hearing aids and using anti-loss strings helps prevent losses, and monthly reviews of incidents show where losses happen.

What if a resident refuses to wear their hearing aids?

Ask why: hearing aids are often refused because they are uncomfortable, whistling, too loud, blocked with wax or not set for the resident's hearing. Try them at the times that matter most, such as visits, meals and activities, and ask the audiologist to review the fit and settings.

When is an ear or hearing problem urgent?

Contact the nurse in charge and the GP or NHS 111 the same day for ear pain, discharge or bleeding, sudden hearing loss, or sudden dizziness. Call 999 if hearing loss comes with signs of stroke such as facial drooping, arm weakness or slurred speech.

What does a Hearing Champion do in a care home?

A Hearing Champion is a member of staff who leads hearing care day to day: encouraging daily hearing aid checks, supporting colleagues, spotting residents who need a hearing assessment, looking after listening equipment, coordinating referrals and building hearing care into care plans. They don't examine ears, remove wax or repair hearing aids.
